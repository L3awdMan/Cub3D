Inside cub3D — bonus build

Module 1 · The big picture

A flat grid pretending to be a world.

cub3D is a tiny first-person 3D maze built on top of a 2D map. There are no polygons, no GPU, no real 3D. Every wall you see in the window is drawn one vertical stripe at a time from a top-down floor plan.

What you actually see when you launch the bonus build

🧱
Textured walls in front of you

Drawn column-by-column by the raycaster. Different XPM image per compass direction.

🗺️
A circular minimap top-left

A top-down disc showing the cells around you, your facing arrow, and your field-of-view cone.

🚪
Sliding doors on the D cells

Press E facing one — it retracts symmetrically from its center, and you can see the room behind it while it's still opening.

🖱️
Mouse-look + WASD movement

The cursor stays visible (so you can still click the X to close), but moving the mouse horizontally rotates your view.

The trick: cast one ray per screen column.

Your window is 1280 px wide. The renderer does not draw triangles — it shoots 1280 imaginary beams straight out from your eyeball into the 2D map, one for each pixel-column on screen. Each beam walks across the grid until it bumps into a wall. The distance it traveled decides how tall that one stripe of wall looks.

💡
Why this still matters in 2026

Raycasting is the canonical first project for understanding 3D graphics from scratch. Modern engines hide cameras, projections, and depth buffers behind layers of abstraction. cub3D forces you to build all three by hand — in roughly 1500 lines of C.

Module 2 · Raycasting math

One beam, one grid, one wall stripe.

Think of the map as graph paper. You're a dot on it, looking in some direction. For each column on the screen, the renderer fires a beam from your dot, walks it cell-by-cell across the graph paper until it bumps into a wall cell, and asks: "how far did you go?" That distance becomes the height of a vertical stripe on the screen. Do that 1280 times and you have a frame.

The cast of characters in t_ray

Each beam carries its own little notebook of variables as it walks the grid. Before we touch the math, learn the words.

🎯

dir_x, dir_y

Where the beam is pointing. A unit-ish vector built from the player's facing + a sideways shove for this particular column.

📍

map_x, map_y

The integer grid cell the beam is currently sitting in. Bumped by ±1 each DDA step.

📏

delta_x, delta_y

How far the beam travels to cross one full cell on the X axis (or Y axis). Constant for the whole trip.

📐

side_dist_x, side_dist_y

Distance from the player to the next vertical grid line (or next horizontal grid line). Updated every step.

🧭

step_x, step_y

+1 or −1 per axis. Tells the DDA which direction to step the cell index in.

🪞

side

0 if the last hop crossed a vertical grid line, 1 if it crossed a horizontal one. Decides which texture face was hit.

Step 1 — Aim the beam (init_ray)

The screen column you're rendering (left edge, middle, right edge) decides how much the beam tilts off your forward direction. The player has two vectors: dir (forward) and plane (a perpendicular sideways vector whose length defines the FOV). The beam direction for column x is simply dir + plane × camera_x, where camera_x remaps the column to the range −1 → +1.

CODE — ray_utils.c:22

	ray->map_y = (int)p->pos_y;

	else

	if (ray->dir_y == 0)
		ray->delta_y = 1e30;

📐
Why |1 / dir_x|?

If the beam moves dir_x units along X for every 1 unit of beam-length, then it needs 1 / dir_x units of beam-length to advance by 1 unit of X. That's exactly how far we have to travel to cross from one vertical grid line to the next. Absolute value because we only care about distance, not direction.

Step 3 — Walk until you hit something (the DDA loop)

DDA is one of those algorithms that sounds intimidating and turns out to be five lines. The whole idea: at every step, look at side_dist_x vs side_dist_y. Whichever is smaller is the nearer grid line. Cross it (bump the cell index, add the delta to that side_dist), and ask: did I land on a wall?

Step 4 — Real distance, no fisheye (calc_draw_params)

The naive "Euclidean distance from player to hit point" produces a curved fisheye image — corners stretch outward. The fix is to use only the perpendicular component of the hit distance, measured along the player's forward direction. That's side_dist − delta on the axis that was crossed last. From there, screen height is just WIN_H / perp_dist: closer wall → bigger stripe.

🐟
Why perpendicular distance fixes fisheye

If you used Euclidean distance, rays at the edge of your view would be longer than rays in the center even for the same flat wall — that's a bowed image. By measuring only the component along your forward axis, every column of a flat wall reports the same depth, and the wall renders flat.

Step 5 — Where on the texture did we hit? (calc_wall_x)

Now we know which face of which wall got hit. We still need to know where on that face — left edge of the brick? right edge? — so we can pick the matching texture column. That's the fractional part of the hit position along whichever axis the beam was sliding along.

There's a sister helper for doors

ray_hit_fraction does the same idea as calc_wall_x, but it runs inside the DDA — before perp_dist is even finalized. The door pass-through test needs the hit fraction the moment the beam enters a 'D' cell, so this helper uses side_dist − delta directly to compute the entry distance instead of trusting perp_dist.

🔁
Why two near-identical helpers?

It looks like duplication but it's an ordering constraint. calc_wall_x runs once per column after the DDA finishes, when perp_dist is set. ray_hit_fraction has to run during the DDA, mid-walk, on every door cell. Same math, different lifecycle. Keeping them separate lets the door code stay decoupled from wall-drawing.

Module 3 · Movement & rotation

A box on graph paper that walks and spins.

The player isn't a model with feet — it's two 2D vectors. dir points where you're facing. plane is a sideways vector perpendicular to dir that defines the FOV. Move the player by adding a scaled copy of either vector to their position. Rotate by multiplying both vectors through a 2D rotation matrix. That's the whole game of movement.

Held-key state, not key events

cub3D doesn't move you on KeyPress. It marks the key as "currently held" and lets the per-frame loop sample that state every time. Smooth movement, no autorepeat lag.

Movement keys are "level-triggered" — read every frame while held. Toggles (door, minimap) are "edge-triggered" — only the moment the key goes from up to down counts. The !cub->keys[key] check before flipping is the edge gate.

Here's the subtle bit. The proposed new X and new Y are checked separately. If X is fine but Y is into a wall, you slide along X. That's why you can rub against a wall and slip around it instead of stopping dead.

🧱
Why split the X and Y collision tests?

If you tested the new (X, Y) as a single point, hitting a wall would stop you cold. Testing X and Y independently means you keep whichever component didn't hit, so you smoothly slide along the wall. Every shooter does this; cub3D's version is six lines.

Rotation is a single matrix multiply

To rotate by angle α, both dir and plane are multiplied through the same 2D rotation matrix. They stay perpendicular because the same orthogonal transformation is applied to both.

∑
The 2D rotation matrix

new_x = old_x · cos(α) − old_y · sin(α)
new_y = old_x · sin(α) + old_y · cos(α)

Apply it to both your direction vector and your camera plane. The pair stays perpendicular because rotation preserves angles.

CODE — rotation.c:28 
void	rotate_vectors(t_player *p, double angle)
{
	double	old_dir_x;
	double	old_plane_x;
	double	cos_a;
	double	sin_a;

	cos_a = cos(angle);
	sin_a = sin(angle);
	old_dir_x = p->dir_x;
	p->dir_x = old_dir_x * cos_a - p->dir_y * sin_a;
	p->dir_y = old_dir_x * sin_a + p->dir_y * cos_a;
	old_plane_x = p->plane_x;
	p->plane_x = old_plane_x * cos_a - p->plane_y * sin_a;
	p->plane_y = old_plane_x * sin_a + p->plane_y * cos_a;
}

⚠️

Notice old_dir_x being saved before the first overwrite. If you skip that and compute dir_y using the just-updated dir_x, the second equation reads the wrong value and your rotation slowly drifts the vector lengths to zero. Same trap as swapping two variables without a temp.

Module 4 · Mouse look

Catch every pixel of horizontal travel. Spin the view.

Mouse-look in cub3D is three small ideas glued together. The motion hook just collects raw pointer movement into a counter. A focus guard prevents giant jumps when you alt-tab back. The per-frame loop drains the counter and feeds it to the same rotate_vectors the keyboard uses. No cursor warp, no cursor hide, no DPI surprises.

The design constraint: don't break the X close button

A lot of FPS engines hide the cursor and re-center it every frame so they can read relative motion. That breaks two things on Linux/MiniLibX: you can't click the window's × to quit, and refocus from the terminal sends one wild motion event with hundreds of pixels of "delta" from wherever the cursor sat in the meantime.

🖱️
Three rules

1. Never warp the cursor. 2. Never hide the cursor. 3. The first motion after focus only seeds the baseline — it does not rotate.

State lives in t_cub

mouse_last_x The X coordinate of the previous MotionNotify event. Used to compute the delta.
mouse_dx Accumulated horizontal pixels since the last frame. Drained to zero every frame.
mouse_ready Latch. 0 means "next motion event just seeds the baseline." Cleared on focus loss.

The motion hook accumulates deltas

MotionNotify (X event 6) fires for every pixel the mouse moves over the window. The hook does nothing fancy — it just subtracts the previous X from the current X and adds the result to the running total.

⚙️
Why accumulate, not "use last event"?

Multiple MotionNotify events can land between two frames — a fast flick might fire 30 events in 16 ms. If you only used the most recent delta you'd throw away 29/30 of the motion. The accumulator captures all of it; the per-frame drain rotates by the total.

Module 5 · Doors

A door that retracts from its middle — and lets rays through the slit.

The door is a 1×1 cell tagged 'D' in the map. It has one number — progress, from 0.0 (closed) to 1.0 (fully open) — and one target. Each frame the progress eases toward its target. The clever part: while the door is mid-open, the renderer lets rays pass through the retracted middle band and stop on the still-solid sides. That's how you can see the room behind a half-open door and still see the door's edges sliding away.

The key idea: a sliding center band

Picture the door face as a 1-unit-wide strip. The two halves of the door panel slide apart from the middle. At progress p, a slit of total width p sits centered on the door face — the open band is the interval [0.5 − p/2, 0.5 + p/2]. Any ray that hits that band should pass through. Any ray that hits the still-solid sides should stop and render the door texture.

🚪
The band math

hit ∈ ( 0.5 − p/2 , 0.5 + p/2 ) → pass through
else → panel hit

At p = 0 the band is empty — door fully solid. At p = 1 the band is the whole face — door fully open. Linear in between.

Module 6 · Minimap

A circular cookie cutter stamped onto a square grid.

The minimap is built as a square in memory — cells, player marker, FOV cone, white ring — but every single pixel write is gated by one Euclidean distance test against the disc center. Outside the disc? silent drop. Inside? written. That's the entire technique. No alpha mask, no second image, no MLX trickery.

The geometry

MM_X, MM_Y Top-left screen position of the minimap bounding box. (16, 16) — tucked under the corner.
MM_SIZE Bounding-box side length in pixels. 108.
MM_R Disc radius. 54 = half of MM_SIZE.
center (MM_X + MM_R, MM_Y + MM_R) = (70, 70). The center of the disc on screen.
MM_CELL Pixels per map cell drawn on the minimap. 9.
MM_RADIUS How many cells in each direction from the player are visible. 6.

The cookie cutter — mm_in_circle

For a candidate pixel (x, y), compute its offset (lx, ly) from the disc center. If lx² + ly² ≤ MM_R², the pixel is inside. Plain integer arithmetic — no sqrt, because comparing squared distances against squared radius gives the same answer faster.

{

	int	ly;

	lx = x - (MM_X + MM_R);
	ly = y - (MM_Y + MM_R);
	return (lx * lx + ly * ly <= MM_R * MM_R);

The condition distance ≤ R is equivalent to distance² ≤ R² whenever both sides are non-negative. Squaring is one multiply; sqrt is a much more expensive transcendental. For a 108×108 box at 60 FPS, that adds up.

Module 7 · Shade & head bob

Three tiny tricks that make flat pixels feel like a corridor.

Walls are textured. Doors slide. The minimap shows you a disc of map. None of that, by itself, feels three-dimensional. Three small effects close the gap: depth fog (far things go dark), a North/South darkening trick from Wolfenstein, and a sine-wave head bob that rocks the horizon while you walk. Each is a few lines of math.

The per-channel multiplier — shade_rgb

Almost every shaded pixel in the game is just texel × k, where k is a number between 0 and 1. Multiply each of the R, G, B channels independently by k, clamp back to [0, 255], repack. Black at k = 0, original color at k = 1.

CODE — shade.c:25 
unsigned int	shade_rgb(unsigned int color, double k)
{
	int	r;
	int	g;
	int	b;

	r = (int)(((color >> 16) & 0xFF) * k);
	g = (int)(((color >> 8) & 0xFF) * k);
	b = (int)((color & 0xFF) * k);
	if (r < 0) r = 0;
	if (g < 0) g = 0;
	if (b < 0) b = 0;
	if (r > 255) r = 255;
	if (g > 255) g = 255;
	if (b > 255) b = 255;
	return ((unsigned int)((r << 16) | (g << 8) | b));
}

Why channels independently?

A pixel color is three numbers in one int: red, green, blue. Scaling them in lockstep dims the whole color uniformly toward black. Scaling them differently would tint the color — useful for sunset effects, but cub3D wants plain darkening.

Trick 1 — Depth fog

Far walls should be dark, close walls bright. The formula is simply fog = min(1, FOG_K / perp_dist). At perp_dist ≤ FOG_K = 6 (six cells away) the wall is at full brightness. Beyond that, brightness falls off as 1 / distance. Multiply the texel by that and you've got fog.

Trick 2 — Wolfenstein side darkening

Faces hit on the X axis (East/West) and faces hit on the Y axis (North/South) get different brightness. Same texture, but one set rendered at 0.55 × brightness. The eye reads that as a corner — adjacent walls look like adjacent walls, not one continuous flat sheet. Wolfenstein 3D did this in 1992 and it still works.

🧱
Why this sells "corner-ness"

In real life, light from above falls more directly on horizontal-facing walls than on perpendicular ones. The brain expects the contrast. Without side darkening, two adjacent textured walls bleed into one ambiguous shape; with it, the corner pops.

This module is the engine that turns a world position (x, y) into pixels. Everything in the next four modules (enemies, AI, combat, pickups) rides on top of it.

The three jobs, every frame

draw_sprites() runs after the wall pass, because it needs cub->zbuf — the per-column wall depth the raycaster just filled in. Each frame it does exactly three things, in order.

1
Sort far-to-near

sort_sprites() orders the array so distant sprites are drawn first and near ones paint last, on top. Classic painter's algorithm.

2
Transform to camera space

sprite_transform() projects each world position into a screen column + depth + on-screen rectangle, using the inverse of the camera matrix.

3
Blit column by column

draw_sprite_cols() walks the rectangle, drops any column the wall is in front of (z-test), and copies texels for the rest.

The maths bit — sprite_transform

This is the one piece of real linear algebra in the whole sprite system, and it's worth getting. The camera is described by two vectors: dir (where you're looking) and plane (the camera plane, perpendicular to dir, that sets the field of view). Together they form a 2×2 matrix that maps camera space → world space. To draw a sprite we need the opposite: world → camera. So we invert the matrix.

Why depth is the perpendicular distance, not the straight-line distance

Exactly like walls, the sprite's height comes from its distance along the camera direction, not the raw Euclidean distance. The z-buffer — why walls hide sprites

The raycaster left a wall depth for every screen column in cub->zbuf[x]. A sprite column is only drawn if the sprite is nearer than the wall in that exact column: s->depth < cub->zbuf[x]. That single comparison is the entire occlusion system — an enemy behind a pillar is silently cut off column by column, and you never see it bleed through.

Module 9 · Enemies & the bestiary

A character in the map text becomes a living, animated monster.

The sprite engine from the last module can draw a card in the world. Now we fill the world with things to shoot. Module 10 · Enemy AI

See the player, walk toward the player, shoot the player — on a cooldown.

The AI is deliberately simple, and that's a strength — it's easy to defend and easy to reason about. Once per frame, every enemy runs one tick: end any finished firing pose, take a step toward you if it can see you, and open fire if you're in range and its cooldown has elapsed. No pathfinding graph, no behaviour trees — just line of sight, distance checks, and a couple of timers.

The driver — one tick per enemy per frame

update_enemies() grabs the wall-clock once and ticks every sprite. The real logic is enemy_tick(), and reading it top to bottom is the AI spec.

Line of sight — a thin marched ray

An enemy shouldn't shoot you through a wall. los_clear() answers "can this enemy see the player?" by walking a point from the enemy toward you in steps of 0.1 cell, asking is_blocked() at each step. The first wall (or closed door, or off-grid) returns 0 — no shot. Reach the player with nothing in the way and it returns 1.

{
	dx = cub->player.pos_x - s->x;
	dy = cub->player.pos_y - s->y;

The two flashes — post-process vignettes

Both flashes run after the whole frame is drawn, tinting cub->img directly. They're full-screen pixel scans, but they only run during the brief fade — when no flash is active the function returns immediately, so there's no cost in normal play.

🩸

Damage flash

Reddens the screen, strongest at the corners (edge_factor = squared distance from centre). Alpha ramps from HURT_MAX_A to 0 over HURT_FADE_MS since player.hurt_ms.

💚

Pickup flash

Softly adds green (meat) or gold (money) in a FLASH_EDGE_PX border band only. Fades from FLASH_MAX_A over FLASH_FADE_MS since flash_ms.

💡
The flashes are mutually exclusive — newest wins

You can't be red and green at the same time. The damage flash bails if flash_ms > hurt_ms; the pickup flash bails if hurt_ms >= flash_ms. Whichever event happened most recently is the one that draws. A clean way to arbitrate two effects that share the screen without blending into mud.

Reward pickups close the loop

Kills drop loot (Module 11): a normal enemy drops SP_MEAT, the boss drops SP_MONEY. collect_rewards() runs each frame, and when you walk within REWARD_PICK_DIST of one, reward_effect() applies it: meat heals MEAT_HEAL (clamped at full) and flashes green; money flashes gold.

🏆
Grabbing the money bag is the win condition

Look closely at reward_effect: collecting SP_MONEY doesn't just flash gold — on floor 3 it starts the final cutscene (CUT_END_F3), and on any other floor it calls enter_state(cub, GS_WIN). So the whole game loop is: kill the boss → it drops money → you grab it → you win the floor. That hand-off into the game-state machine is the subject of the next module.

Module 13 · Game flow & cutscenes

One integer decides whether you're in a menu, playing, dead, winning, or watching a movie.
